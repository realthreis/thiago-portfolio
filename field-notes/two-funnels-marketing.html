Marketing Now Has Two Funnels — And Most Teams Are Only Building One

A human buying committee, and the AI systems now doing the first round of vendor research on that committee's behalf.

The job has split into two funnels running in parallel, and most marketing organizations have only noticed one of them. The first is the funnel everyone still plans around: a human buying committee moving through awareness, evaluation, and decision. It's touched by ads, content, sales conversations, and events. The second is newer and largely invisible in most planning documents. It's the AI systems, search summaries, and increasingly autonomous agents. They do the first round of vendor research on that committee's behalf, before a human ever opens a comparison spreadsheet.

Both funnels are real. Both convert into pipeline. A marketing team optimized entirely for the first one is ceding a growing share of the buying process to a system. It isn't building anything for that system.

What changed, specifically

A few years ago, "how a buyer finds you" meant two things. First, a search results page largely under a company's own control through SEO. Second, a set of channels, like social, email, and events, where a human decided what to click. That's not gone, but it's no longer the whole picture. A growing share of enterprise buyers now start vendor research inside an AI assistant. They ask it to summarize a category, shortlist options, or explain the difference between two named competitors. The assistant answers using whatever it can find, cite, and trust, with no vendor in the room to correct a bad summary.

This is a different kind of visibility problem than SEO ever was. Ranking on a search results page rewarded keyword coverage and backlink authority. Being cited accurately by an AI answer engine rewards something closer to what good journalism has always rewarded. Specific claims, verifiable data, named sources: things an algorithm can quote with confidence rather than paraphrase into mush. Generic marketing copy, the kind built from adjectives instead of evidence, doesn't just underperform with this second funnel. It's often invisible to it, because there's nothing specific enough in it to cite.

Volume stopped being the constraint

For most of the last decade, more content generally beat less content. The limiting factor on most marketing teams was production capacity: writers, designers, budget. That constraint has largely dissolved. AI tools have collapsed the cost of producing content, research summaries, and campaign variations. Every competitor in every category can now produce roughly infinite content at roughly the same quality floor.

When production capacity stops being scarce, it stops being a differentiator. What's scarce now is distinctiveness. Positioning specific enough to survive being paraphrased by a person or summarized by a machine. Proof specific enough that a skeptical buyer, or an AI system doing due diligence, can't wave it away as an unverifiable claim. The second piece in this series covers the same discipline from the positioning side. Copy that could describe three other vendors was already a problem when a human read it. It's a worse problem when the reader is a system with three other vendors' equally vague copy to choose from instead.

What building for both funnels actually looks like

The instinct, once a team notices the second funnel exists, is to treat it as a technical SEO problem. Structured data, schema markup, a checklist for the web team. Some of that helps at the margins, but it misses where the real leverage is. AI answer engines cite sources that carry genuine authority on a specific claim: original data, named customer results, a benchmark nobody else has published. That's not a markup problem. It's a content-strategy problem, and it's the same content strategy that wins with human enterprise buyers doing careful research. Proprietary data and credible proof now serve two audiences with the same asset, instead of one.

Account-based strategy and answer-engine visibility turn out to be less separate than they first appear. A well-built benchmark report, aimed at the accounts identified through the kind of tiering discussed in the first piece in this series, does double duty. It's a legitimate reason to re-engage a named account. It's exactly the kind of citable asset that earns a mention when someone, human or AI, asks a general question about the category. Building for one funnel well tends to strengthen the other. That holds as long as the underlying asset is genuinely specific, not generic content produced at scale.

What this means for how teams should be built

None of this argues for bigger marketing teams. If anything, it argues for smaller ones doing more consequential work. AI has made content, research, and campaign operations far cheaper to produce. The honest answer to "what should our team spend its newly freed-up time on" isn't more content, faster. It's two things AI still can't do on its own. First, deciding which claims are true and differentiated enough to be worth making. Second, building the kind of original proof, like data, case studies, and named-customer results, that both a skeptical buyer and an AI system will trust.

That's a judgment call, account by account and claim by claim, not a workflow to automate. Teams that treat it as a workflow will keep producing content nobody, human or machine, has a reason to believe. Teams that treat it as judgment will find the same discipline wins pipeline in both funnels at once. It was never really two different problems. It was one problem, being specific and credible enough to be worth citing, now showing up in two places that used to be one.
