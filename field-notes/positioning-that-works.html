What Actually Makes Product Positioning Work (And Why Most of It Doesn't)

Strip the company name off the page. If the copy underneath could describe two or three other vendors, the positioning work hasn't been done yet.

Here's a test I run on any positioning statement, mine or anyone else's. Strip the company name off the top of the page. If the copy underneath could describe two or three other vendors in the category, the positioning work hasn't been done yet. What's left is description: a list of things the product does. Positioning is a claim about why this specific option is the right one, stated in a way nothing else in the category can honestly copy.

Most positioning fails this test, and it's rarely because the writing is bad. It's because positioning gets treated as a copywriting exercise, something to polish after the real decisions have already been made elsewhere. It should be a decision in its own right.

Positioning names the problem, not the product

The strongest positioning I've been part of building never started with the product. It started with naming the buyer's problem more precisely than the buyer could name it themselves. The language was theirs, not vendor language dressed up to impress.

The distinction sounds subtle, but it isn't. Vendor language describes capabilities: what the product does, how it works, what's under the hood. Buyer language describes consequences: what happens if this problem doesn't get solved, what it's currently costing them, what they've already tried that didn't work. A buyer who reads positioning and thinks "that's exactly it, the thing I've been trying to explain to my boss" has just been sold to. No feature list sells that effectively. You've handed them their own argument back, sharper than they had it.

Getting there means knowing the problem at the level the buyer experiences it, not the level a product spec describes it. That's not a positioning-team exercise done at a desk. It comes from field time: sitting with the buyer's actual workflow, hearing the same frustration come up unprompted. Across multiple conversations, the same objections keep recurring in lost deals. Positioning built without that input is positioning built from assumption. Assumption-built positioning is exactly what produces copy that could describe three other vendors.

It has to draw a specific line

Naming the problem well is necessary but not sufficient. Real positioning draws a clear line against the alternative. That's often a named competitor, or just as often the status quo: a workaround the buyer cobbled together because nothing purpose-built existed yet.

Vague contrast is where a lot of otherwise-good positioning goes soft. "More powerful," "easier to use," "built for scale." These are comparative words without a comparison attached. They read as confident but commit to nothing, which means they can't be disproven, which means they can't be believed. Specific contrast is riskier to write and far more persuasive to read. It names what the buyer is actually choosing between, and stakes a claim a competitor would have to directly contest.

This is uncomfortable for a lot of organizations, because specific contrast requires an explicit choice about what you are not. That decision belongs to leadership, not a copywriter. It's usually the real reason positioning stalls in committee. Nobody has been willing to make the underlying choice the sentence would have to reflect.

The repeatability test

The last check I run comes after naming the problem and drawing the line. Does the positioning survive being repeated by someone other than the person who wrote it? If a sales rep, a customer, or a new hire can't say it back correctly after hearing it once, it isn't positioning yet. It's a slide that only works in the room where it was presented.

This matters more than it sounds. Positioning that needs the original author's tone or supporting slides to land isn't operational, it's a performance. Positioning that survives being paraphrased in a hallway conversation will propagate through an organization. It shows up in sales calls, in support tickets, in how a customer describes you to their own boss. That propagation is the entire point. Positioning nobody outside the marketing team can repeat isn't positioning at all. It's a document.

Why this discipline matters more now, not less

There's a temptation to treat positioning as a solved problem, something every category leader already has and newer companies can defer. The opposite is closer to true. Buying research increasingly runs through search results and AI-generated summaries before a human ever sees a sales deck. Vague or interchangeable positioning doesn't just underperform with a human reader anymore. It becomes invisible to the systems doing the summarizing. An answer engine synthesizing "how is this different" across a category can only surface a specific, well-drawn contrast. It has nothing to work with when every vendor's positioning says the same thing in different words.

Writing now has two audiences reading in parallel, one human and one algorithmic. That's reshaping what good marketing means in 2026, the subject of the final piece in this series. The account tiering from the first piece tells you who to reach and when. Positioning is the raw material that determines whether reaching them accomplishes anything once you do.
