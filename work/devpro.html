Rebuilding a Global Outstaffing Unit Around New Business

Full P&L ownership of a global staff augmentation business unit — connecting Latin American engineers to U.S. enterprise demand, with growth rebuilt around new-logo acquisition and healthier margins.

Context

Dev.Pro is a U.S.-headquartered software company. Alongside its core software-project development business, it ran a global staff augmentation ("outstaffing") unit that placed engineers — primarily from Latin America — directly into client teams. I was brought in to run that unit as a standalone global business: full P&L, commercial strategy, delivery accountability, regional expansion, and the bridge between Latin American talent supply and U.S. enterprise hiring demand. I reported into Dev.Pro's commercial leadership.

The challenge

The unit's revenue sat in an inherited client portfolio at inherited margins. Staff augmentation is also a commoditised category — buyers compare rate cards first and everything else second. Growing the unit meant doing two hard things at once: opening genuinely new enterprise accounts, and signing them at better economics than the book we started from.

What I did

Owned the unit's P&L as a global business Commercial strategy, pricing, delivery accountability, and the operational frameworks for regional expansion, client engagement, and retention — run as one business with one number, not a set of disconnected accounts.
Built the team from scratch Hired across Recruitment, Sales, and Customer Success to stand up an 11-person business unit — the operating capacity the growth targets depended on.
Rebuilt growth around new-logo acquisition In the first year the unit won nine new enterprise logos and grew the active contractor base by more than 30%, instead of relying on expansion inside the accounts it inherited.
Proved the land-and-expand motion, not just new logos Active customer success turned two of the nine new accounts into expansions within their first two months — additional contracts on top of the original scope. The unit could win accounts and grow them.
Moved the economics, not just the volume New business was signed at margin rates roughly 20% above the legacy portfolio — more than US$1M in new annualised contracted revenue in year one.
Put AI into the funnel where judgment still made the call Used AI tooling to detect ICP buying signals and to speed up sourcing and candidate matching, while keeping the decision on which signals and which matches actually belonged in the pipeline a human one.
Ran enterprise RFPs end to end Coordinated the full RFP response process for the Gates Medical Research Institute — qualification, solution shaping, and proposal.
Positioned the unit past the rate-card conversation Built go-to-market messaging that led with delivery outcomes and domain fit rather than hourly arbitrage, to separate Dev.Pro from undifferentiated staffing vendors.

What this built

This was a 0-to-1 assignment inside an established company: take a unit running on inherited accounts and stand up a real business around it — an 11-person team, a new-logo pipeline, and a working land-and-expand motion. In July 2026, Dev.Pro made a portfolio decision to wind the unit down and concentrate exclusively on its core software-project development business — a company-level call about where to focus, separate from how the unit itself was tracking.

How a business unit performs and which businesses a parent company chooses to be in are two different questions. I owned the first. The second sat above the role — and with new logos, a bigger base, and better margins on the board, that's a clean place to leave it.
